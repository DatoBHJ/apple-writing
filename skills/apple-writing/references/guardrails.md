# Guardrails — where Apple’s style is illegal, irresponsible, or simply wrong

Read this before writing anything in a **regulated, adversarial, or apologetic** surface. The style in this skill is Apple’s; the liability in this file is yours.

Apple’s confident register is defensible **because Apple footnotes it and can afford the lawyers**. Copy the punchline without the machinery and you have copied the part that gets companies fined, sued, and corrected by regulators.

---

## 1. The four hard boundaries

Route *before* you write. If the text is any of these, the marketing register is not available to you.

| Surface | Boundary | What to do instead |
|---|---|---|
| **Pharmaceutical / medical claims** | 21 CFR 202.1 requires the qualification to be “comparable in depth and detail” to the claim — the qualification must be as prominent as the claim it qualifies. A hero line with a small-print footnote is **structurally illegal** here, not merely risky. | Lead with the indication and the limitation. No superlatives. No withheld qualification. | 
| **Financial, credit, insurance, health, children’s data, EU/UK GDPR** | The regulator’s standard is *comprehensibility* — GDPR Art. 12(1) requires information “in a concise, transparent, intelligible and easily accessible form, using clear and plain language”. Ornamental ambiguity is the failure mode. | Plain editorial register. Hedge everything you cannot evidence. Name the condition, the exception, and the time limit in the sentence. |
| **Adversarial / legal / regulatory surfaces** — a dispute, a takedown, a complaint response, a lawsuit, an incident | Confidence reads as evasion. Apple’s own privacy register is quoted **against** it in litigation: the DOJ complaint describes Apple as wrapping “itself in a cloak of privacy, security, and consumer preferences” and deploying them as “an elastic shield” (US v. Apple, D.N.J. 2:24-cv-04055 ¶16). The more quotable the sentence, the more quotable it is against you. | Factual, dated, attributable. State what is true, what is not claimed, and who decided. No brand adjectives at all. |
| **Apology / failure / outage / defect** | **Apple has no apology register.** Its famous iPhone 4 letter normalises the defect, reframes it as a display bug, reasserts the superlative (“the best we have ever shipped”), and apologises for the reader’s feelings. That is issue management, not accountability. Do not imitate it. | Say what broke, who it affected, what is being done, and by when. Do not reframe the defect, do not restate the product’s excellence, do not apologise for anyone’s feelings. |

## 2. A claim and its qualification are one unit

The FTC’s guidance for advertisers is the sharpest statement of the rule this skill teaches:

> “When practical, advertisers should **incorporate relevant limitations and qualifying information into the underlying claim**, rather than having a separate disclosure qualifying the claim.”
> — *FTC, .com Disclosures: How to Make Effective Disclosures in Digital Advertising* (2013), verified in the original PDF

Apple’s own practice is the **opposite** of the FTC’s preference: the hero carries the claim, and a numbered footnote below the fold carries the qualification. Apple gets away with it at Apple’s scale, with Apple’s legal review. The FTC’s test is whether the disclosure is “clear and conspicuous” and “unavoidable” — which a footnote under three screens of bento tiles is not.

**So, in order of preference:**
1. Put the limitation **inside the sentence**: `Up to 24 hours of battery life, depending on how you use it.`
2. If it cannot fit, put it in the **same visual block**, not below the fold.
3. Only then a numbered footnote — and never for a claim you could not defend without it.

The pattern has a real price: a US class action over advertised-but-undelivered features turned on exactly this structure, alleging that no disclaimer contradicted the “prominent Challenged Representations” (*Landsheft v. Apple Inc.*, N.D. Cal. 5:25-cv-02668 ¶30).

## 3. Absolutes are promises

`never`, `always`, `the only`, `the first`, `the best`, `guaranteed`, `100%`, `no one else` — each is a **falsifiable public commitment** with an expiry date. Apple’s privacy sentences are the most beautiful in its corpus and the most dangerous to copy, because a single exception (a subsidiary, a partner, a feature shipped later) turns the sentence into evidence.

**Test before you publish any absolute:** who could disprove this with one example? If the answer is “anyone with a support ticket”, rewrite it with the hedge Apple itself uses in its footnotes — `not all devices are eligible`, `varies by use and configuration`, `available on iPhone 15 and later`.

## 4. Dose, scale and parody

Measured from Apple’s own pages: **16% of headlines** carry a superlative or absoluteness marker (13 of 83), each one footnoted, spaced across a long page. That is the dose.

- Three or four superlatives in a paragraph do not multiply the effect — they cancel it, and the reader discounts all of them.
- **At most one stylistic device per paragraph.** A fragment, a rule of three, and a superlative in the same paragraph is not Apple; it is a parody of Apple, and readers recognise it instantly.
- Apple itself stops short of the strongest words: across 83 headlines there were **zero** uses of `magical` as a product claim. The word that survives is the milder product name — `Magic Keyboard` — and one pun.

**Anti-caricature test:** remove every adjective and superlative from your text. If what remains still says something specific and true, you were writing. If nothing remains, you were decorating.

## 5. Wordplay does not travel — and neither does confidence

Two measured findings, both with consequences for anything you write:

- **Coined compounds, puns and withheld-verb fragments are the least portable devices.** Apple’s Korean and Japanese copy is *transcreated*, not translated — the joke is rebuilt, not carried (`Fast runs in the family.` → `스피드는 칩안 내력.`; `Happily ever faster.` → `기승전빠름.`). The two-beat `Label. Payoff.` shape survives translation; a pun does not. If the text will be localized, keep the structure and drop the wordplay.
- **Confidence is earned, not stylistic.** Apple’s register assumes an audience that already trusts the sender. On a first impression, for a small or unknown brand, or on a subject involving someone’s loss, money, health or culture, the same sentences read as arrogance. Apple has itself been criticised and has apologised for advertising that read its audience’s culture as a punchline (the 2024 Bangkok *Underdogs* film; the *Crush!* iPad advertisement).

---

## 6. What is *not* a boundary

- **Plain language is never the problem.** GDPR asks for plain language; the FTC asks for clear disclosure; accessibility guidance asks for the same. Warmth and clarity are compatible with every regime here.
- **Contractions, `you`, short sentences and concrete nouns are safe everywhere.** The invariant core in `SKILL.md` §2 does not create liability in any regime.
- **The boundary is ambiguity and unearned certainty** — not the register itself. The honest rule is: *no ambiguity where ambiguity creates liability, and no absolute claim you cannot footnote.*

---

## 7. Quick routing check

Before writing, answer these four:

1. **Who can be harmed if this sentence is wrong?** (Nobody → marketing register is available. Somebody’s money, health, safety or rights → editorial register only.)
2. **Could one example disprove this sentence?** (Yes → hedge it now, not later.)
3. **If this text were read aloud in court, would it help or hurt?** (Hurt → remove every adjective and state the facts.)
4. **Is this a surface where someone is already upset?** (Yes → accountability register: what happened, what it means, what is being done, by when. No reframing, no excellence, no jokes.)
